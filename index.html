“快”科研联合声明

——致各位导师与科研工作者

（本声明由学生发起，也欢迎所有认同其理念的科研工作者联署）

我们是一群来自国内外高校与科研院所的本科生、研究生、博士后与青年科研工作者，大多从事计算机科学、人工智能及相关领域的研究。我们共同签署这份声明，想从学生的视角，和各位导师与同行说几句真心话。

一、我们认同的，与我们想补充的

不久前，许多导师联署了《学生“慢”科研联合声明》，提醒我们不要以论文数量为目标，要把问题想深、把工作做透。我们读后深受触动，也由衷认同：灌水不是科研，挂名不是贡献，思考无可替代。

但作为身处其中的学生，我们也想说出另一半真实：慢本身不是美德，快本身也不是过错。质量的反面不是速度，而是草率与无法验证。一个正确的结果早一年被提出、被检验、被开源，整个领域就能早一年站在它的肩膀上前进。科学的进步，本身就需要速度。

二、我们无法回避的现实

有些话，我们平时很少说出口：

  1. 竞争是真实的，我们无法单方面退出。 在人工智能这样日新月异的领域，同一个想法常常有多个团队在同时推进。晚几个月，一整年的心血就可能沦为“同期工作”，甚至被评价为“缺乏新意”。科学界历来以优先权分配荣誉——这不是虚荣，而是规则本身。
  2. 我们的时间有明确的边界。 本科阶段真正能投入科研的时间往往只有一两年，博士阶段的资助通常也只有三到五年；奖学金、签证与合同都有固定期限，不少院校仍有硬性的发表毕业要求；评奖、实习、升学、求职与教职申请都有不等人的截止日期，也都要看发表记录。对博士后与青年教师而言，“非升即走”的考核同样有一只从不停摆的时钟。
  3. 等待，占据了科研中太多的时间。 一次投稿从提交到结果往往需要三到六个月；一篇扎实的工作，可能因为与其正确性无关的原因被拒两三次，一年多就此过去。NeurIPS 在 2014 年与 2021 年进行的两次一致性实验都发现：约一半被录用的论文，换一组独立的审稿人就可能被拒。工作在等待中变旧，我们在等待中毕业。
  4. 慢的代价，往往由最没有安全感的人承担。 对已获长聘的教授而言，慢下来的一年是一种选择；对学生而言，这一年可能意味着资助中断、错过求职季，或被他人抢先发表。只要评价体系不变，单方面劝学生“慢下来”并不能改变竞争本身，只会把代价留给最无力承担的人。也正因如此，我们格外珍视那些承诺从评价方式上做出改变的导师。
  5. 工具已经改变了科研的节奏，且不会倒退。 借助开源基础设施与 AI 工具，过去需要数周的实验如今可能只需几天，文献调研与写作也在加速。问题不再是科研要不要变快，而是我们验证质量、认可贡献的机制能否跟上。当写出一篇论文的成本越来越低，验证一篇论文的能力，就成了科学真正的瓶颈。

三、我们的核心观点：快与好并不对立

速度与质量并不天然矛盾。清晰的问题、严谨的设计、可复现的代码与诚实的实验，恰恰是一项工作又快又好的前提。科研中真正耗时的，往往不是深入的思考，而是等待：等反馈、等算力、等审稿、等下一个截稿日。我们想缩短的是等待，而不是思考。

学术认可在很大程度上是一场零和博弈：审稿人的精力、会议的录用名额、读者的时间、职位的数量与评审委员会的信任，都是有限的。在这场博弈中，真正的对立不在“快”与“慢”之间，而在“高质量的快”与“低质量的快”之间。每一篇仓促、无法验证或拆分凑数的论文，都在挤占高质量工作应得的注意与名额，也让所有“快”的工作蒙上怀疑，最终让所有人都变得更慢。因此，我们抵制低质量的快，正是为了保护高质量的快，让它得到应有的认可。

我们也不赞成“既然评审机制终将被洪流冲垮，不如推波助澜”的想法。用低质量的洪流冲垮评审，最先被淹没的，恰恰是那些认真做事、却没有名气与资源兜底的学生。我们想要的是更好的机制，而不是更大的混乱。

我们抵制的快

  • 为赶截稿或抢占先机，把未经充分验证的结论匆忙投出
  • 把一项工作拆成多篇，以数量代替贡献
  • 选择性报告结果，把指标调到“刚好超过”基线
  • 隐藏代码与关键细节，让他人无从复现
  • 批量生成缺乏实质贡献的论文
  • 挂名而不参与，署名而不负责

我们支持的快

  • 问题清晰，主张可验证，证据充分
  • 代码、数据与实验细节公开，可被快速复现
  • 诚实报告局限、失败与负面结果
  • 尽早公开，主动接受同行检验
  • 尽早识别走不通的方向，及时止损
  • 先完成，再完美：完整走完一次研究，再把下一次做得更好

把时间留给思考，而不是等待。

四、科研最需要“快”起来的地方

  1. 质量验证要快，而且要可靠。 当投稿量以万计，我们不能把对科学质量的判断，全部寄托于几位时间有限、经验不一的匿名审稿人在几周内给出的意见。我们并不责怪审稿人——他们中的许多人，正是和我们一样在繁重科研之余义务审稿的学生；问题在于机制。我们呼吁建立能够快速检验质量的机制：随投稿提交代码与实验产物并进行可复现性检查，在统一的环境中独立评测核心结果，要求审稿意见指向具体证据，加强审稿人的培训与问责，并公开评审记录。让质量可以被快速检验，而不只是被缓慢地评判。
  2. 评审周期要快。 我们希望更多会议与期刊采用滚动投稿、快速初审、由同一组审稿人跟进修改的机制，而不是让一篇已经修改完善的工作，一次次回到队尾从头再来。
  3. 反馈与决策要快。 一份草稿等上几周，一个实验方案等到下个月的组会，一个方向在犹豫中拖了半年——这些等待对学生的代价，远比看起来更大。我们希望与导师约定清晰的反馈节奏，也希望导师在一个方向不值得继续时尽早直言。一句及时的“这个方向不值得做”，胜过三个月的沉默。
  4. 起步要快：让第一次机会来得更早。 许多学生想进入科研，却先被要求拥有项目经历、熟练的研究技能，甚至已发表的论文。如果第一次机会只留给已经证明过自己的人，经验又从何而来？我们希望实验室为新人提供有指导、范围清晰的入门课题，鼓励他们在有限的时间里完整走完一次研究：提出问题、设计方法、开展实验、写成论文、接受检验。先完成，再完美——一项真实而完整的工作，往往比一个停留在构想中的宏大课题更能让人成长。我们也呼吁高校、科研机构与企业提供面向学生的小额科研经费、开放算力与短期研究项目，为年轻人的试错留出空间。
  5. 对高质量工作的认可要快。 我们希望以预印本时间戳公正认定优先权，不因评审期间出现的同期工作而否定一篇论文的贡献；也希望在毕业、招聘与评奖中，开源代码、独立复现与实际应用等可验证的质量证据，能与发表的会议名称一样被认真看待。
  6. 纠错要快。 科学的速度，不只取决于发表得有多快，也取决于错误被发现和纠正得有多快。我们希望复现报告、勘误与公开讨论得到鼓励，指出错误的人受到尊重，主动更正自己的错误被视为一种担当，而非污点。

五、我们的承诺与请求

作为联署者，我们承诺：

  • 不以牺牲质量换取速度：让每一个核心结论都有可复现的证据支撑，尽可能公开代码、数据与实验细节，诚实报告局限与失败。
  • 作为审稿人，认真、准时地审稿；对不确定的判断，坦承不确定。
  • 尊重同期工作，公正引用他人的贡献。
  • 尽早寻求反馈，尽早暴露问题。
  • 当我们有能力时，也为后来的新人提供第一次机会。

我们也恳请各位导师与同行：

  • 以可验证的质量——而不是论文数量，也不是速度本身——来评价我们。
  • 与我们约定及时反馈的节奏；在工作的核心结论经过充分验证后，支持我们及时公开，而不必为追求“完美”无限期等待。
  • 给新人第一次机会，并在有指导的前提下，容许他们快速试错。
  • 理解资助、签证、毕业与求职节点给我们带来的真实约束。
  • 在担任领域主席、程序委员、编辑与评审委员时，与我们一起推动更快、更可靠的质量验证机制。

我们想要的不是更快的论文，而是更快的科学。快，是为了让好的工作更早被检验、被看见、被使用，也让更多年轻人更早开始、更快成长；而不是让未经检验的东西更早被发表。

Joint Statement on Quick Research

To Supervisors and Fellow Researchers

Initiated by students, and open to every researcher who shares its views.

We are undergraduates, graduate students, postdocs, and early-career researchers at universities and research institutes around the world, most of us working in computer science, artificial intelligence, and related fields. We sign this statement to share a few honest words, from a student’s perspective, with our supervisors and colleagues.

I   What We Agree With, and What We Want to Add

Not long ago, many supervisors signed the Joint Statement on Slow Research, urging students not to chase paper counts but to think deeply and work thoroughly. We were moved by it, and we agree: padding a publication list is not research, honorary authorship is not contribution, and nothing replaces careful thought.

But as students who live inside this system, we also want to tell the other half of the story. Slowness is not a virtue in itself, and speed is not a fault. The opposite of quality is not speed; it is carelessness and unverifiability. When a correct result is proposed, tested, and open-sourced a year earlier, the whole field can build on it a year earlier. Scientific progress itself depends on speed.

II   Realities We Cannot Wish Away

There are things we rarely say out loud:

  1. Competition is real, and we cannot opt out of it alone. In a field moving as fast as AI, the same idea is often pursued by several teams at once. A delay of a few months can turn a year of work into “concurrent work,” or get it dismissed as “lacking novelty.” Science has always allocated credit by priority. That is not vanity; it is how the system works.
  2. Our time has hard limits. Undergraduates often have only a year or two to spend on research, and PhD funding usually lasts three to five years. Scholarships, visas, and contracts all have fixed terms, and many institutions still require publications for graduation. Fellowships, internships, graduate admissions, job applications, and faculty searches all have deadlines that do not wait, and all of them look at publication records. For postdocs and junior faculty, the up-or-out clock never stops ticking.
  3. Too much of research is spent waiting. A submission typically takes three to six months to reach a decision. A solid paper can be rejected two or three times for reasons unrelated to its correctness, and a year or more slips away. Two consistency experiments run by NeurIPS, in 2014 and 2021, both found that about half of the accepted papers would have been rejected by an independent set of reviewers. Our work grows stale while it waits, and we graduate while we wait.
  4. The cost of slowness falls on those with the least security. For a tenured professor, a slow year is a choice. For a student, it may mean losing funding, missing the job market, or being scooped. As long as evaluation systems stay the same, asking students alone to slow down does not change the competition; it only leaves the cost with those least able to bear it. That is exactly why we so value the supervisors who have committed to changing how students are evaluated.
  5. Tools have changed the pace of research, and there is no going back. With open-source infrastructure and AI tools, experiments that once took weeks may now take days, and literature review and writing are accelerating too. The question is no longer whether research will get faster, but whether our mechanisms for verifying quality and recognizing contributions can keep up. As the cost of producing a paper falls, our capacity to verify papers becomes the real bottleneck of science.

III   Our Core View: Fast and Good Are Not Opposites

Speed and quality are not inherently in conflict. A clear question, a rigorous design, reproducible code, and honest experiments are precisely what allow work to be both fast and good. Much of the time research consumes goes not into deep thinking but into waiting: for feedback, for compute, for reviews, for the next deadline. What we want to shorten is the waiting, not the thinking.

Academic recognition is, to a large extent, a zero-sum game: reviewers’ attention, acceptance slots, readers’ time, open positions, and committees’ trust are all finite. In this game, the real divide is not between fast and slow, but between high-quality speed and low-quality speed. Every rushed, unverifiable, or artificially sliced paper takes attention and slots that high-quality work deserves, and casts suspicion on all fast work, until in the end everyone becomes slower. We resist low-quality speed precisely to protect high-quality speed, and to make sure it receives the recognition it deserves.

Nor do we accept the idea that since peer review will be swept away anyway, we might as well help the flood along. When review is drowned in low-quality work, the first to go under are the students who work carefully but have no reputation or resources to fall back on. We want better mechanisms, not more chaos.

Speed we reject

  • Rushing under-verified conclusions out the door to meet a deadline or plant a flag
  • Slicing one piece of work into several papers, trading contribution for count
  • Reporting results selectively, tuning numbers until they “just beat” the baseline
  • Withholding code and key details so that no one can reproduce the work
  • Mass-producing papers with no substantive contribution
  • Taking authorship without taking part, or credit without responsibility

Speed we stand for

  • Clear questions, verifiable claims, and sufficient evidence
  • Open code, data, and experimental details that allow quick reproduction
  • Honest reporting of limitations, failures, and negative results
  • Sharing early and inviting scrutiny from peers
  • Recognizing dead ends early and cutting losses
  • Done before perfect: completing one full study, then making the next one better

Save our time for thinking, not for waiting.

IV   Where Research Most Needs to Speed Up

  1. Verifying quality must be fast, and reliable. When submissions number in the tens of thousands, we cannot leave every judgment of scientific quality to a few anonymous reviewers with limited time and uneven experience, working within a few weeks. We do not blame reviewers; many of them are students like us, reviewing voluntarily on top of their own research. The problem is the mechanism. We call for ways to verify quality quickly: submitting code and artifacts with reproducibility checks, evaluating core results independently in shared environments, requiring reviews to point to concrete evidence, strengthening reviewer training and accountability, and making review records public. Quality should be quickly testable, not only slowly judged.
  2. Review cycles must be fast. We hope more conferences and journals will adopt rolling submissions, fast initial decisions, and revisions followed by the same reviewers, instead of sending an already improved paper back to the end of the queue to start over.
  3. Feedback and decisions must be fast. A draft waits weeks for comments; an experiment plan waits for next month’s group meeting; a direction drifts for half a year in indecision. These delays cost students far more than they appear to. We hope to agree with our supervisors on a clear rhythm of feedback, and we hope supervisors will tell us early when a direction is not worth pursuing. A timely “this isn’t worth doing” is better than three months of silence.
  4. Getting started must be fast: first chances should come sooner. Many students who want to do research are first asked for project experience, polished research skills, or even publications. If first chances go only to those who have already proven themselves, where is that experience supposed to come from? We hope labs will offer newcomers well-scoped, well-supervised starter projects, and encourage them to complete one full research cycle within a limited time: posing a question, designing a method, running experiments, writing it up, and putting it up for scrutiny. Done comes before perfect. One real, complete piece of work often teaches more than a grand project that never leaves the planning stage. We also call on universities, research institutes, and companies to offer students small research grants, open compute, and short-term research programs, leaving room for young people to try and fail.
  5. Recognition of high-quality work must be fast. We hope priority will be fairly established by preprint timestamps, and that a paper’s contribution will not be dismissed because concurrent work appeared during review. We also hope that in graduation, hiring, and awards, verifiable evidence of quality, such as open code, independent reproductions, and real-world use, will be weighed as seriously as the name of a venue.
  6. Error correction must be fast. The speed of science depends not only on how fast we publish, but on how fast errors are found and corrected. We hope reproduction reports, errata, and open discussion will be encouraged; that those who point out errors will be respected; and that correcting one’s own mistakes will be seen as integrity rather than a stain.

V   Our Commitments and Our Requests

As signatories, we commit to the following:

  • We will not trade quality for speed. Every core claim we make will be backed by reproducible evidence; we will release code, data, and experimental details whenever possible, and report limitations and failures honestly.
  • As reviewers, we will review carefully and on time, and acknowledge uncertainty when we are uncertain.
  • We will respect concurrent work and credit others fairly.
  • We will seek feedback early and surface problems early.
  • When we are able to, we will give the newcomers after us their first chance.

We also ask our supervisors and colleagues:

  • To evaluate us by verifiable quality: not by paper counts, and not by speed itself.
  • To agree with us on a timely rhythm of feedback, and, once the core conclusions of our work are well verified, to support sharing it promptly rather than waiting indefinitely for perfection.
  • To give newcomers a first chance, and to let them try and fail quickly, with guidance.
  • To understand the real constraints that funding, visas, graduation, and the job market place on us.
  • When serving as area chairs, program committee members, editors, and evaluators, to work with us toward faster and more reliable ways of verifying quality.

We do not want faster papers. We want faster science. Speed matters so that good work can be tested, seen, and used sooner, and so that more young people can start earlier and grow faster; not so that untested work can be published sooner.
